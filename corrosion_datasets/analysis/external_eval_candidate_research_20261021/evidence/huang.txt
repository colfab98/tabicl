corrosion through large-scale literature mining and 
database construction

Abstract
Pitting corrosion is one of the most prevalent and detrimental forms of localized corrosion in stainless steels 
exposed to chloride-containing environments. Its initiation and propagation are governed by the combined 
effects of alloy composition, environmental conditions, and experimental parameters. Although a substantial 
body of experimental data has been accumulated through decades of research, these data remain largely dis -
persed across the scientific literature, limiting their accessibility for large-scale statistical analyses and data-
driven investigations. To address this challenge, this study proposes a large language model (LLM)-based 
framework for structured data extraction and database construction from scientific literature. By decomposing 
the complex information extraction process into multiple subtasks, including experimental condition extraction, 
pitting potential extraction, and data alignment, the framework enables the automated acquisition and inte -
gration of experimental data from unstructured scientific documents. Using the proposed framework, 12,792 
candidate publications retrieved using a broad stainless-steel pitting corrosion search strategy were processed, 
resulting in a structured extraction output containing 36,095 experimental records. The database encompasses 
key information, including alloy compositions, environmental parameters, and pitting potentials. Stainless-
steel-specific descriptive and statistical analyses were subsequently conducted using material-screened subsets 
of the extracted records. Data quality evaluation demonstrates that the proposed framework achieves strong 
aggregate performance in structured information extraction from complex scientific texts, while stage-specific 
validation indicates that pitting-potential extraction and normalization remain more challenging than the over -
all field-level extraction task. Statistical analysis of the resulting database reveals that existing studies are 
predominantly focused on room-temperature, neutral chloride environments, as well as austenitic and duplex 
stainless steel systems. Furthermore, partial correlation analysis revealed conditional associations between the 
investigated variables and pitting potential. Among these variables, molybdenum exhibited the strongest posi -
tive conditional association, whereas temperature showed a negative conditional association. Chloride ion con -
centration showed only a weak negative association. These trends were generally consistent with established 
corrosion knowledge and therefore provided a scientific plausibility check for the statistical patterns observed 
in the constructed database. This work highlights the potential of large language models for knowledge extrac -
tion and automated database construction from corrosion-related scientific literature. The proposed framework

Introduction
Stainless steels are widely utilized in critical sectors, including marine engineering, energy infrastructure, chemi-
cal processing facilities, transportation systems, and nuclear power plants, owing to their excellent mechanical 
properties, manufacturability, and corrosion resistance 1–3. In chloride-containing environments, a passive film 
formed on the stainless steel surface effectively isolates the metallic substrate from corrosive media, thereby 
providing the material with superior corrosion resistance. However, when the passive film is locally disrupted 
by chloride ion attack, highly localized corrosion pits may initiate on the material surface. These pits can sub -
sequently promote the development of more severe degradation processes, such as crevice corrosion, stress 
corrosion cracking, and corrosion fatigue 4–6. Due to its concealed nature, rapid propagation, and potentially 
catastrophic consequences, pitting corrosion is widely regarded as one of the most detrimental forms of localized 
corrosion affecting stainless steels during service7,8.
Numerous studies have demonstrated that the pitting corrosion behavior of stainless steels is governed by the 
combined effects of alloy composition, microstructural characteristics, and environmental conditions. Among 
these factors, alloying elements such as chromium (Cr), molybdenum (Mo), and nitrogen (N) are widely rec -
ognized as key contributors to enhanced pitting corrosion resistance and form the basis of the classical Pitting 
Resistance Equivalent Number (PREN) model 9–11. Meanwhile, environmental parameters, including tempera -
ture, chloride ion concentration, pH, scan rate, and electrolyte composition, have also been shown to significantly 
influence passive film stability and localized corrosion processes 12–14. For example, increasing temperature 
generally reduces passive film stability and increases susceptibility to pitting corrosion, whereas elevated chlo -
ride ion concentrations accelerate localized passive film breakdown and promote pit initiation 15–17. Although 
extensive experimental investigations have been conducted on individual stainless steel grades or under specific 
environmental conditions, substantial variations in experimental conditions, testing methodologies, and material 
systems often limit the consistency and comparability of reported findings. Consequently, extracting generaliz -
able trends from the vast body of dispersed literature data and establishing a statistical understanding that spans 
different material systems and environmental conditions have become important research objectives in the field 
of corrosion science.
In recent years, the rapid development of Materials Informatics has provided new opportunities for investigat-
ing complex material behaviors18,19. By leveraging high-throughput computation, big data analytics, and machine 
learning techniques, researchers can uncover underlying patterns from large volumes of experimental and com -
putational data, thereby enabling materials property prediction and design optimization 20. However, compared 
with fields such as battery materials, catalytic materials, and structural materials, the corrosion domain remains 
relatively data-deficient. This limitation primarily arises from the fact that corrosion-related experimental data are 
highly fragmented across the scientific literature, with a substantial proportion of critical information embedded 
in textual descriptions, tables, and figures. As a result, such information cannot be readily transformed into struc-
tured datasets suitable for statistical analysis and machine learning applications. This data acquisition bottleneck 
has significantly hindered the development of Corrosion Informatics.
The advancement of Natural Language Processing (NLP) technologies has provided new opportunities for the 
automated extraction of information from scientific literature. Traditional approaches typically rely on keyword 
matching, rule-based extraction, or supervised learning models to perform data extraction tasks. However, these

methods often exhibit limited performance when dealing with complex table parsing, multi-variable relationship 
identification, and cross-paragraph information integration 21. In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs), 
exemplified by the GPT series, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in text comprehension, information 
extraction, and complex reasoning tasks 22–24. Through their ability to capture and interpret contextual seman -
tics, LLMs can automatically identify critical information from unstructured scientific documents, including 
experimental conditions, material compositions, and performance metrics, thereby substantially reducing the cost 
and effort associated with database construction 25–29. At the same time, considerable research efforts have been 
devoted to addressing challenges associated with LLMs, including hallucinations, information loss in long-con -
text processing, and the stability of structured outputs. Various optimization strategies and constraint mechanisms 
have been proposed to mitigate these issues 30–32, thereby providing a more reliable technical foundation for the 
automated extraction of scientific data.
Although LLMs have demonstrated considerable potential in fields such as biomedicine, chemistry, and materi-
als science, their application in corrosion science remains at an early stage. To date, most studies on stainless steel 
pitting corrosion have been conducted as individual experimental investigations, with relatively limited efforts 
devoted to large-scale analyses based on literature-derived datasets. In particular, current understanding of the 
relationships among alloy composition, environmental conditions, and pitting potential is largely derived from 
limited experimental datasets and empirical models, while comprehensive statistical validation across multiple 
studies, stainless steel grades, and environmental conditions remains lacking. Furthermore, substantial variations 
in experimental methodologies, testing conditions, and data reporting practices exist among different studies, 
making traditional manual data curation approaches inadequate for large-scale database construction. Therefore, 
the development of a high-quality database for stainless steel pitting corrosion, together with systematic statisti-
cal analyses based on such a database, is important for advancing data-driven corrosion research.
Motivated by these challenges, this study proposes an LLM-assisted data extraction framework for corrosion 
science literature. By combining task decomposition with a structured output strategy, the framework enables 
the automated extraction and integration of pitting-corrosion-related information from a broad literature corpus. 
Using the proposed framework, 12,792 candidate publications retrieved using a broad stainless-steel pitting cor-
rosion search strategy were processed, resulting in a large-scale structured extraction output containing 36,095 
experimental records. Material-screened subsets were subsequently used for stainless-steel-specific descriptive 
and statistical analyses. The database encompasses key variables, including alloy compositions, experimental 
environmental parameters, and pitting potentials. Building upon this database, data quality assessment, statisti -
cal profiling, and partial correlation analysis were further performed to systematically investigate the conditional 
associations between alloy composition, environmental factors, and pitting potential. The present work not only 
provides a reusable data resource and technical framework for corrosion informatics research but also dem -
onstrates a practical application of Large Language Models for knowledge extraction from materials science 
literature.
Method
In this study, a prompt-based Large Language Model (LLM) framework was employed to extract structured 
experimental data from scientific literature (Fig. 1). Unlike conventional approaches that require training task-
specific natural language processing models, the proposed framework leverages carefully designed prompts to 
guide the LLM during the information extraction process. These prompts explicitly define the extraction objec -
tives, data schema, and output constraints. By incorporating domain-specific instructions into the prompts, the 
model is able to interpret the content of scientific publications and transform unstructured experimental descrip-
tions into structured records.
Prompting strategies can generally be categorized into three types:

(1) Zero-shot prompting33,34, in which only task instructions are provided in the prompt, without any example

(2) Few-shot prompting 35, in which several task-specific examples are included in the prompt to guide the

(3) Chain-of-thought prompting 36, which encourages the model to perform intermediate reasoning based on 
the input before generating the final response, thereby improving its ability to satisfy complex task requirements.
In the present study, a zero-shot prompting strategy was adopted. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the designed prompt 
consists of three main components:
(1) Task instructions, which define the objectives of the information extraction task;
(2) Rule constraints, which specify the conditions and restrictions governing the extraction process;
(3) Output format specifications, which constrain the model to generate structured outputs suitable for subse -
quent automated processing.
LLMs rely on attention mechanisms to dynamically focus on relevant portions of the input text. However, 
when processing lengthy scientific documents containing diverse types of information, the model’s attention 
may become dispersed across irrelevant content, thereby adversely affecting the accuracy of information extrac-
tion37,38. The fundamental principle of the attention mechanism can be expressed as follows:

Considering that this study involves large-scale automated information extraction from more than 12,000 
corrosion-related publications, the LLM configuration was selected by jointly considering scientific literature 
comprehension, structured information extraction capability, extraction accuracy, reliability, inference efficiency, 
and deployment cost. To quantitatively evaluate the model selection, a comparative benchmark was conducted 
using five model configurations: GPT-3.5, Gemini Flash, DeepSeek-v3.2, Qwen-Max, and the proposed Deep -
Seek-v3.2–Qwen-Max hybrid configuration.
To ensure a fair comparison, all model configurations were evaluated using the same preprocessing proce -
dure, task-decomposition workflow, prompt instructions, output schema, and a representative validation subset 
of 100 publications. Model performance was assessed in terms of precision, recall, F1 score, hallucination rate, 
valid-JSON output rate, processing failure rate, average processing time, and approximate processing cost. Con-
dition–result correspondence was additionally examined as part of the record-level correctness assessment. The 
quantitative benchmark results are presented in Sect.  “Results and discussion”.
Based on the comparative evaluation, DeepSeek-v3.2 and Qwen-Max were adopted in a task-specific hybrid 
configuration for large-scale database construction. DeepSeek-v3.2 was employed for experimental-condition 
extraction (Task A), whereas Qwen-Max was used for pitting-potential extraction (Task B) and condition–result 
alignment (Task C). This task-specific allocation was designed to exploit the complementary capabilities of the 
two models while maintaining a practical balance between extraction performance and computational cost.

Document preparation and preprocessing
The source literature corpus used in this study was retrieved from the ScienceDirect database using the keyword 
query “stainless steel pitting corrosion”. The publication period was restricted to 2019–2026, and the document 
type was restricted to research articles. Literature retrieval was completed on April 23, 2026. Based on these 
search criteria, 12,792 publications were retrieved and acquired in Portable Document Format (PDF), and all 
retrieved publications were directly included in the subsequent automated information-extraction workflow with-
out additional manual material-level screening or deduplication.
The literature retrieval stage was intentionally designed as a broad, high-recall search to minimize the risk of 
excluding potentially relevant studies. Because the ScienceDirect keyword search is relevance-based rather than 
an exact material-domain classification, the retrieved corpus could also include publications involving non-stain-
less materials. Accordingly, the 12,792 publications represent the candidate literature corpus used for automated 
information extraction rather than a manually verified set of stainless-steel-only publications.
All publications included in the corpus were peer-reviewed articles published in SCI-indexed journals, pro -
viding a baseline level of quality control for the extracted experimental data. Although journal-level impact and 
ranking may vary, impact factors or journal rankings were not used to weight individual records because these 
bibliometric indicators do not directly reflect the reliability of individual experimental measurements. Therefore, 
all extracted records were treated equally in the subsequent analysis.
To further characterize the application coverage of the literature corpus, the retrieved publications were classi-
fied into broad application-oriented research fields based on application-related keywords identified from the title 
and first-page textual content. As shown in Fig. 3, the corpus covers a diverse range of fields, including manufac-
turing and surface engineering, nuclear and energy, marine and offshore applications, biomedical applications, 
construction and infrastructure, and chemical and process industries, as well as general corrosion and materials 
research. The uneven distribution across these fields reflects differences in research attention among applica -
tion areas. Accordingly, the statistical trends derived from the complete corpus should be interpreted as overall 
cross-literature patterns rather than as relationships that are necessarily equally generalizable to every specific 
application field. The preprocessing workflow began with the parsing of PDF documents to extract experimental 
information presented in both narrative text and tabular formats. The PyMuPDF library was employed to extract 
page-level textual content, converting the original PDF layouts into machine-readable text sequences.

In addition to narrative text, experimental conditions in corrosion-related literature are frequently reported in 
tabular form. Therefore, the pdfplumber library was further employed to extract table data from the PDF docu -
ments. This tool is capable of identifying table structures and retrieving cell-level information. To facilitate subse-
quent processing by Large Language Models (LLMs), each extracted table was converted into a linearized textual 
representation that preserved both the column headers and the row-wise data entries. Under this representation, 
each table row was treated as an individual experimental condition, enabling its direct mapping to a structured 
database record.
Subsequently, the extracted narrative text and the reconstructed table text blocks were merged into a unified 
textual input, enabling the Large Language Model (LLM) to process heterogeneous information sources, includ-
ing descriptive text and tabular data, in a consistent manner. This unified representation served as the input for 
the subsequent prompt-based information extraction workflow. The overall preprocessing and data integration 
procedure is illustrated in Fig. 4.
Prompt-based experimental condition extraction (Task A)
Experimental conditions were extracted using a prompt-based LLM framework. A zero-shot prompting strategy 
was adopted, whereby the model was guided to perform information extraction solely through carefully designed 
natural language instructions without the provision of task-specific examples. To ensure the accuracy and reli -
ability of the extracted results, the prompts explicitly encoded the extraction objectives, schema constraints, and 
extraction rules. The prompt design consisted of three main components: task instructions, rule constraints, and 
output format specifications. This design enabled the LLM to perform controlled information extraction from 
unstructured scientific texts.
Task Instructions: In this study, the LLM was instructed to perform the information extraction task while assum-
ing the role of a domain expert in corrosion science41. A predefined set of target fields was specified in the prompt 
(Fig. 5), corresponding to the schema of the structured database. Each field was accompanied by an explicit defi-
nition, and the model was instructed to extract a value only when the corresponding information was explicitly

All extracted information was mapped to a predefined set of output fields to ensure consistency across different

multiple experimental conditions were reported within a single publication, such as variations in alloy com -
position, solution chemistry, or testing parameters, the model was instructed to generate multiple independent 
records. Each record corresponded to a distinct experimental condition, thereby preserving the one-to-one cor -

illustrated in Fig. 6.
Rule Constraints: Model hallucination refers to the tendency of LLMs to generate information that is not explic-
itly present in the input text but appears contextually plausible or coherent 42. In information extraction tasks, 
hallucinations often manifest as the inference of missing numerical values or the generation of details that are not 
explicitly reported, thereby significantly compromising the reliability of structured datasets.
To mitigate this issue, a set of stringent rule constraints was incorporated into the prompt design43. The model was 
explicitly instructed to extract only information that was explicitly reported in the source text and to avoid any 
form of inference, estimation, or speculation. For parameters that were not reported in the original document, the 
corresponding fields were omitted rather than populated with placeholder values. This constraint-based design 
ensured that all extracted data were evidence-based and directly traceable to the source literature. Consequently, 
the reliability, consistency, and transparency of the resulting dataset were substantially improved.

Output Format Specifications: The extraction output was defined as a JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) array, 
in which each object represented an independent experimental condition. This design enabled multiple experi -
mental settings reported within a single publication to be represented as separate structured records.
To mitigate response truncation and incomplete outputs caused by excessive input–output token lengths, a sparse 
output strategy was adopted. Under this strategy, each JSON object contained only those fields that were explicitly 
reported in the source document, while fields corresponding to missing parameters were omitted. This approach 
effectively reduced the number of output tokens and improved the robustness and consistency of the generated 
results44.
Subsequently, the generated JSON array was parsed into individual database records, and the extracted param-
eters were standardized through unit normalization to ensure consistency across different data sources. Finally, all 
processed records were integrated to construct the structured database of experimental conditions.
Pitting potential extraction (Task B)
In this study, the target parameter was the pitting potential (Epit). The pitting potential, also referred to as the criti-
cal pitting potential, is a key electrochemical parameter used to evaluate the susceptibility of metals to localized 
corrosion. It is defined as the potential at which the passive film undergoes stable breakdown, resulting in the 
initiation of pitting corrosion. In potentiodynamic polarization tests, pitting potential is typically identified as the 
potential corresponding to a pronounced and sustained increase in anodic current density, indicating the onset of 
pit formation. In general, a higher pitting potential value corresponds to greater resistance to pitting corrosion. 
Consequently, this parameter is widely used as an important indicator for comparing the corrosion performance 
of different alloys under various environmental conditions.
In this subtask, a high-recall candidate generation procedure was employed to identify potentially relevant 
text segments, and the overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 7. Candidate blocks were extracted from both nar -
rative text and tabular data. For paragraph-level candidates, filtering was performed based on the co-occurrence 
of corrosion-related trigger terms and potential-value patterns. For table-level candidates, relevant content was 
identified using structural indicators (e.g., “Table X”) together with keyword-based filtering. This rule-based 
strategy ensured that all potentially relevant regions were retained for subsequent analysis.

Subsequently, the filtered candidate blocks were processed using an LLM-based extraction module. The model 
was instructed to identify and extract pitting-related potential values that were explicitly reported in the source 
text and to return the results in a structured JSON format. To address ambiguity in terminology and reporting 
conventions, the extracted results were categorized into two groups: epit_records, representing high-confidence 
pitting potential values, and epit_candidates, representing potential candidates with weaker textual evidence or 
whose interpretation depended on the surrounding context. These candidate entries were subjected to further 
evaluation in subsequent processing stages.
To ensure data reliability, an evidence-based information extraction strategy was adopted. Each extracted value 
was required to be supported by a corresponding evidence text (*evidence_text*), which contained both the rel -
evant terminology and the associated numerical value. The model was explicitly instructed to avoid any form of 
inference, estimation, or speculation, thereby ensuring that all extracted data remained directly traceable to the 
original literature.
Finally, the extracted results underwent a post-processing stage for validation and standardization. This proce-
dure included the removal of invalid entries, standardization of numerical representations and units, and identifi-
cation of the reference electrodes associated with the reported pitting potentials, including SCE, Ag/AgCl, SHE, 
and RHE. To improve comparability across studies, pitting-potential values were subsequently harmonized to 
the saturated calomel electrode (SCE) scale. For records in which the reference electrode was not retained in the 
structured extraction output, the corresponding source publications were re-examined to recover the reference-
electrode information; these records were confirmed to have been measured versus SCE and were therefore 
treated as SCE-referenced potentials. Potentials reported against other identifiable reference electrodes were 
converted to the SCE scale using the corresponding reference-electrode potentials. Records for which a reliable 
conversion could not be established were excluded from analyses requiring harmonized pitting-potential values.

Data alignment and structured database construction (Task C)
In corrosion studies, a single publication often investigates multiple experimental conditions and consequently 
reports multiple corresponding experimental outcomes. Therefore, accurately aligning these results with their 
associated experimental conditions is a critical step in the construction of a reliable structured database.
Traditional approaches based on natural language processing or deep learning typically require large volumes 
of annotated data or rely on complex feature engineering to capture the relationships between experimental con-
ditions and experimental outcomes. In contrast, LLMs possess strong contextual understanding and reasoning 
capabilities, enabling them to infer the associations between experimental conditions and their corresponding 
results directly from the original text. These capabilities make LLMs particularly well suited for condition–out -
come alignment tasks in scientific literature.
In this study, an evidence-guided LLM-based alignment strategy was developed to associate the extracted 
experimental condition records with their corresponding pitting potential values. The alignment framework con-
sisted of the following components, as shown in Fig. 8:
Alignment inputs and evidence construction
The alignment process integrated three categories of inputs: (1). Structured experimental condition records 
extracted in Task A; (2). Structured pitting potential records extracted in Task B; (3). Page-level textual evidence 
retrieved from the original PDF documents.
To reduce irrelevant contextual information and improve alignment efficiency, only pages located in the vicin-
ity of the extracted pitting potential entries were selected as supporting evidence. This localized evidence retrieval 
strategy helped focus the model’s attention on relevant content while controlling the input token length.
Constraint-guided alignment using large language models
A constraint-guided alignment strategy based on Large Language Models (LLMs) was employed in this study. 
For each experimental condition record, the model was permitted to select at most one corresponding pitting 
potential record from the outputs generated in Task B. If no reliable match could be established, a null value was 
returned.

The alignment process was performed in an evidence-guided manner, requiring the model to rely on explicit 
textual evidence to justify each mapping relationship. In particular, when pitting potential values were reported 
in tabular form, the model was required to reconstruct the table structure from the flattened PDF text, identify the 
relevant columns (e.g., pitting potential columns), and establish the mapping between sample identifiers and the 
reported numerical values.
When explicit identifiers, such as sample labels, were available, they were used as the primary matching crite-
ria. In the absence of such identifiers, secondary attributes were utilized to infer the corresponding relationships, 
including alloy composition, solution chemistry, temperature, scan rate, and heat-treatment conditions.
To ensure the reliability of the alignment results, the model was restricted to selecting from existing pitting 
potential records and was explicitly prohibited from generating new numerical values. When multiple candidate 
matches existed and insufficient evidence was available to support a unique mapping, the alignment result was 
conservatively assigned as null. This strategy effectively reduced the risk of erroneous associations and improved 
the overall reliability of the constructed database.
Post-processing and database integration
The alignment outputs were generated in a structured format, including the index of the selected pitting potential 
record and the confidence level associated with each mapping. Subsequently, the aligned pitting potential values, 
together with their corresponding units and metadata, were merged back into the experimental condition records 
obtained from Task A.
Finally, all successfully aligned records were integrated to construct the complete structured database, in which 
each record contained both the experimental condition information and its corresponding corrosion performance 
parameter (Epit). Through this process, heterogeneous information extracted from scientific literature was trans -
formed into a unified, machine-readable dataset.

To quantify the statistical uncertainty of the performance estimates while accounting for the non-independence of 
multiple records extracted from the same publication, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for precision, recall, and F1 
score were estimated using publication-level bootstrap resampling. In each bootstrap iteration, publications were 
sampled with replacement, and all records associated with each selected publication were retained as a cluster. 
Precision, recall, and F1 score were recalculated for each resampled dataset. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 
the resulting bootstrap distributions were used as the lower and upper bounds of the 95% CIs.
To provide a more diagnostic assessment of extraction reliability beyond conventional precision, recall, and F1 
score, extraction errors were further classified into eight mutually exclusive categories according to their primary 
error mechanism: (1) hallucinated value, (2) wrong field assignment, (3) incorrect unit, (4) incorrect reference-
electrode representation, (5) table row or column misalignment, (6) incorrect condition–result association, (7) 
duplicated record, and (8) missed value. Each identified error was assigned to a single category according to a 
predefined priority-based classification scheme to avoid double counting.
The error counts used in this diagnostic taxonomy are distinct from the value-level FP and FN events used to 
calculate precision and recall. A single underlying extraction error may contribute to both an FP and an FN in the 
value-level evaluation. For example, when an incorrect value is extracted in place of the corresponding ground-
truth value, the incorrect prediction contributes an FP, while the unmatched ground-truth value contributes an 
FN. In contrast, the same underlying error is counted only once in the diagnostic error taxonomy and is assigned 
to a single primary error category. Consequently, the sum of FP and FN events is not expected to equal the total 
number of errors reported in the error-attribution analysis.
Accordingly, the “missed value” category in the diagnostic taxonomy represents omission-type errors and is 
not numerically equivalent to the total number of FN events. FN events may also arise when a value is extracted 
but fails to match the corresponding ground-truth value because of an incorrect extraction, representation, or 
association (see Table 2).

may manifest at, or be attributable to, more than one processing stage, the total number of stage-level error occur-
rences is not expected to equal the number of unique underlying errors classified by error type in Table 7. The 
resulting error-attribution percentages therefore describe the distribution of component-level error occurrences 
across the pipeline and should not be interpreted as component-specific error rates, because the number of eligible 
evaluation opportunities differed among components.
Treatment of missing outputs: To distinguish genuine source-level missingness from extraction failure, missing 
outputs were evaluated against the manually verified source during validation. When the corresponding informa-
tion was not explicitly reported in the source, the missing field was treated as source not reported rather than as an 
extraction error. When the information was explicitly reported but was not captured or was incorrectly extracted 
by the pipeline, the case was classified as not detected or wrong extraction, respectively. Parsing- and alignment-
related failures were separately identified through the stage-specific error-attribution analysis described above. 
This distinction ensured that source-level absence was not conflated with missingness or errors introduced by the 
extraction pipeline.

Evidence-grounding accuracy was defined as the proportion of extracted values for which the corresponding sup-
porting evidence passage contained both the correct parameter and the correct numerical value. During execution 
of the framework, supporting evidence passages were generated at the relevant subtasks to guide extraction and 
condition–result association; however, these intermediate outputs were not persistently retained after completion 
of the full extraction workflow. Consequently, a retrospective field-level evidence-grounding accuracy could 
not be consistently calculated for the expanded validation set. Grounding reliability was therefore additionally 
assessed through manual comparison with the source-verified ground truth and the unsupported-extraction analy-
sis described above.
Condition–result alignment accuracy was defined as the proportion of eligible condition– Epit pairs in which 
the extracted pitting potential was associated with the correct alloy, specimen, and experimental condition. This 
metric was calculated for the 140 eligible condition–result pairs in the expanded validation set. Because the num-
ber of eligible cases was relatively limited, the resulting accuracy estimate was interpreted cautiously and was 
supplemented by the separate error-attribution analysis of incorrect condition–result associations.
Statistical analysis of the constructed database : For the stainless-steel-specific statistical analysis, mate -
rial-domain screening was performed at the experimental-record level before complete-case selection. Records 
explicitly identifiable from the extracted material information as non-stainless materials, including carbon/low-
alloy steels, aluminum alloys, Ni-based alloys, and other clearly non-stainless metallic materials, were excluded. 
A conservative exclusion strategy was adopted: records were excluded only when the available material infor -
mation explicitly indicated a non-stainless material; otherwise, they were retained for subsequent analysis. Fol -
lowing material-domain screening and reference-electrode harmonization, complete-case selection was applied. 
Records were included only when the SCE-harmonized pitting potential and all variables included in the partial-
correlation analysis contained valid numerical values; records containing missing values in any of these fields 
were excluded. This resulted in 1,379 records from 448 publications for the final partial-correlation analysis.
Individual experimental records were retained as the analytical units, while the dependence among multiple 
records originating from the same publication was accounted for using publication-clustered bootstrap resam -
pling. In each of 2,000 bootstrap iterations, publications were sampled with replacement, and all records associ-
ated with each selected publication were retained as a cluster. Partial correlation coefficients were recalculated 
for each resampled dataset, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting distributions were used to obtain 
publication-clustered 95% confidence intervals. No additional observations were excluded solely on the basis of 
statistical outlier thresholds.

Results and discussion
Comparative benchmark and model selection
To quantitatively determine the LLM configuration for large-scale database construction, five model configura -
tions were comparatively evaluated using a representative validation subset of 100 publications. The same pre -
processing procedures, task-decomposition workflow, prompt instructions, output schema, and evaluation criteria 
were applied to all configurations to ensure a controlled comparison. The benchmark results are summarized in 
Table 3.
As shown in Table 3, the proposed hybrid configuration achieved the highest overall extraction performance, 
with a precision of 91.30%, recall of 91.14%, and F1 score of 91.22%. Among the individual models, Qwen-
Max showed the highest F1 score (88.43%), followed by DeepSeek-v3.2 (87.00%), Gemini Flash (84.81%), and 
GPT-3.5 (78.63%). Compared with Qwen-Max and DeepSeek-v3.2 used independently, the hybrid configuration 
improved the F1 score by 2.79 and 4.22% points, respectively. The improvement was also accompanied by a 
reduction in unsupported extractions, with the unsupported-extraction rate decreasing to 5.01%, compared with 
6.72% for Qwen-Max and 7.83% for DeepSeek-v3.2.
The improved extraction performance of the hybrid configuration was achieved at the expense of longer pro -
cessing time. Its average processing time was approximately 50 s per publication, compared with 28 s for Qwen-
Max and 30 s for DeepSeek-v3.2. Nevertheless, the processing cost remained competitive at ¥0.05 per publication, 
which was lower than that of Qwen-Max (¥0.07), Gemini Flash (¥0.09), and GPT-3.5 (¥0.08), and only slightly 
higher than that of DeepSeek-v3.2 (¥0.04). These results indicate that the proposed hybrid configuration does not 
provide an advantage in inference speed but offers a favorable trade-off between extraction accuracy, hallucina -
tion control, and processing cost. Accordingly, the DeepSeek-v3.2–Qwen-Max hybrid configuration was selected 
for the subsequent large-scale database construction.
Condition–result alignment accuracy was not reported as a standalone quantitative metric because this metric 
is only applicable to publications containing multiple experimental conditions with independently distinguishable 
condition–Epit pairs. Such cases were relatively limited in the 100-publication benchmark subset, and the result-
ing percentage would therefore be highly sensitive to a small number of individual mismatches. Instead, condi -
tion–result correspondence was incorporated into the record-level correctness assessment, whereby an extracted 
Epit value was considered correct only when it was assigned to the corresponding experimental condition.
In addition to the performance metrics reported in Table 3, the operational reliability of the evaluated con -
figurations was assessed in terms of structured-output validity and processing stability. Across all five model 
configurations evaluated on the 100-publication benchmark, the valid-JSON output rate was 100%, defined as the 
proportion of model responses that could be successfully parsed and conformed to the predefined JSON schema.

Stage-specific performance and error analysis
Stage-specific performance differed across the individual components of the extraction pipeline. Task A achieved 
a precision of 94.64%, recall of 93.79%, and F1 score of 94.21%, indicating reliable extraction of experimental 
conditions, material composition, and microstructural information. In comparison, Task B showed lower perfor-
mance for pitting-potential extraction, with a precision of 66.88%, recall of 69.54%, and F1 score of 68.18%. 
For subsequent post-processing, the exact accuracies of unit representation, Epit value/unit normalization, and 
reference-electrode representation were 94.39%, 66.43%, and 90.21%, respectively. Condition–result alignment 
achieved an accuracy of 75.00% among 140 eligible records. After integration of all extracted and processed 
fields, the final database achieved an aggregate precision of 93.83%, recall of 93.26%, and F1 score of 93.54%, 
indicating strong overall field-level extraction performance. However, this aggregate performance should not be 
interpreted as representing equivalent accuracy for each individual variable. In particular, the lower Task B F1 
score, normalization accuracy, and condition–result alignment accuracy indicate that pitting-potential extraction 
and processing remain more challenging than the overall field-level extraction task. This distinction is particularly 
relevant because pitting potential serves as the outcome variable in the subsequent corrosion-statistical analysis. 
Because the evaluation units and eligible denominators differed among components, the stage-specific metrics 
were used to characterize the performance of individual processing functions rather than for direct numerical 
comparison across stages (see Table 4 and Table 5).

Validation of extraction performance
The extraction performance of the proposed framework was evaluated using the expanded manually validated 
dataset described in Sect.  “Validation and statistical evaluation”. Across the validation set, 10,828 true-positive 
(TP) values, 712 false-positive (FP) values, and 783 false-negative (FN) values were identified based on com -
parison with the manually established ground truth. These counts resulted in a precision of 93.83%, a recall of 
93.26%, and an F1 score of 93.54%.
Publication-level bootstrap resampling was further used to quantify the uncertainty associated with these per-
formance estimates. The resulting 95% confidence intervals were 93.14–94.49% for precision, 92.68–93.80% for 
recall, and 92.98–94.06% for the F1 score. The relatively narrow confidence intervals indicate limited uncertainty 
in the estimated extraction performance across the validated publications.
To further characterize extraction reliability beyond the aggregate precision, recall, and F1 score, all identified 
extraction errors were analyzed according to the eight predefined error categories described in Sect.  “Validation 
and statistical evaluation”. The resulting error distribution is summarized in Table 7.
A total of 1,125 underlying extraction errors were identified and classified according to their primary error 
mechanism. Hallucinated values constituted the largest error category, accounting for 596 cases (52.98% of all 
errors) and affecting 125 publications. Missed values represented the second-largest category, with 463 cases 
(41.16%) across 108 publications. Together, hallucinated and missed values accounted for 94.13% of all identi -
fied errors, indicating that the dominant failure modes were unsupported extraction and omission rather than 
formatting or unit-related errors.
Errors related to condition–result association were substantially less frequent, with 46 cases (4.09%) identi -
fied across 20 publications. Incorrect units, table row or column misalignment, and incorrect reference-electrode 
representation accounted for 1.07%, 0.62%, and 0.09% of the errors, respectively. No wrong-field assignments or 
duplicated records were identified in the validated sample.
In addition to the error composition, unsupported-extraction and omission rates were calculated separately to 
distinguish unsupported generation and complete omission from other extraction errors. Among 11,540 extracted 
values, 596 could not be verified against the manually established ground truth, corresponding to an unsup -
ported-extraction rate of 5.16%. Among 11,611 eligible ground-truth values, 463 explicitly reported values were 
completely omitted by the pipeline and were therefore classified as “missed value” errors, corresponding to an 
omission rate of 3.99%. This omission count represents complete extraction failures and is therefore lower than 
the total number of FN events, which also includes ground-truth values that were not correctly matched because 
of other extraction errors. A standalone evidence-grounding accuracy was not retrospectively reported because 
the intermediate supporting evidence passages generated during extraction were not persistently retained after 
completion of the pipeline.
The observed reduction in recall can be attributed to several factors. First, to ensure the reliability of the 
constructed database, the prompting strategy employed stringent extraction constraints. When encountering 
ambiguous or insufficiently supported information, the model tended to adopt a conservative extraction behav -
ior, prioritizing precision over recall. Consequently, potentially relevant data were occasionally omitted rather

overall field-level extraction task. These results support the utility of the framework for automated database 
construction in corrosion informatics while also highlighting the need to account for field-specific extraction 
uncertainty when interpreting downstream analyses.

Notably, the statistical association of Mo observed in this study is greater than that of nitrogen (N), which 
differs to some extent from the weighting assigned to N in the classical Pitting Resistance Equivalent Number

This discrepancy may arise from the fundamental difference between statistical relationships derived from large-
scale literature data and the assumptions underlying idealized alloy design frameworks. The PREN equation is 
essentially an empirical formulation developed for specific stainless steel grades and representative chloride-
containing environments, emphasizing the theoretical strengthening effects of alloying elements on pitting cor -
rosion resistance. In contrast, the present study is based on a large collection of literature data spanning multiple 
stainless steel grades, experimental systems, and testing conditions. Consequently, the observed correlations 
reflect the overall statistical associations of alloying elements at a cross-study level rather than their idealized 
theoretical weighting. In addition, the database coverage of nitrogen (N) is relatively limited (13.51%), whereas 
molybdenum (Mo) exhibits substantially higher coverage (30.23%). As a result, high-nitrogen stainless steels 
constitute only a relatively small fraction of the overall dataset, which may partially reduce the apparent statisti-
cal association of N in the correlation analysis. Furthermore, the influence of N on pitting corrosion resistance 
is often strongly dependent on its metallurgical state, heat-treatment history, and nitride precipitation behavior. 
Consequently, its effect may vary considerably across different material systems and experimental conditions. 
In contrast, the beneficial effect of Mo on pitting corrosion resistance tends to be more consistent across a wide 
range of stainless steel systems. This greater stability may explain why Mo exhibits a stronger overall association 
in the large-scale cross-system statistical analysis performed in this study.
Nitrogen (N) nevertheless exhibits a strong positive partial correlation coefficient (0.284), indicating that it 
remains an important factor influencing the pitting corrosion resistance of stainless steels. Nitrogen is generally 
recognized for its ability to enhance austenite stability, promote chromium enrichment within the passive film, 
and improve local repassivation capability. Consequently, high-nitrogen stainless steels often exhibit elevated pit-
ting potential values47. The results of this study suggest that, although the statistical association of N is lower than 
that of Mo in the large-scale literature-based analysis, it remains one of the most influential alloying elements for 
enhancing pitting corrosion resistance.

Chromium (Cr) exhibits a moderate positive partial correlation coefficient (0.147). As the principal alloying 
element responsible for the formation of the passive film, Cr plays a fundamental role in determining the pitting 
corrosion resistance of stainless steels 48. However, because most stainless steels inherently contain relatively 
high Cr contents, the variation in Cr concentration across the database is comparatively limited. As a result, its 
statistical association in the partial correlation analysis is lower than those of Mo and N.
The observed effects of the major alloying elements are generally consistent with previous experimental stud-
ies on stainless steel pitting corrosion. In particular, the positive association of Mo with pitting potential agrees 
with its well-established role in enhancing passive-film stability and suppressing localized dissolution during pit 
initiation and propagation46,49–50. Similarly, the effects observed for Cr and N are broadly consistent with their 
established contributions to passivation and pitting resistance, which also form the basis of conventional pitting-
resistance indices such as PREN51. However, the magnitudes of the associations observed in the present database 
are relatively modest compared with trends often reported under controlled experimental conditions. This differ-
ence is expected because the present analysis integrates heterogeneous stainless-steel grades, alloy compositions, 
solution chemistries, temperatures, and electrochemical testing conditions across a large number of independent 
studies. Therefore, the present results should be regarded as cross-literature statistical tendencies rather than 
direct substitutes for relationships derived from controlled experiments.
Among the environmental variables, temperature exhibits a clear negative partial correlation coefficient 
(− 0.139), indicating that increasing temperature is associated with a reduction in pitting potential. This observa-
tion is consistent with established theories of pitting corrosion. Elevated temperatures can accelerate chloride ion 
(Cl⁻) transport and anodic dissolution processes while reducing the stability of the passive film, thereby facilitat-
ing the initiation and stabilization of corrosion pits52.
Manganese (Mn) also exhibits a negative partial correlation with pitting potential (− 0.105). This observa -
tion may be closely associated with the presence of MnS inclusions. MnS inclusions are widely recognized as 
important initiation sites for pitting corrosion in stainless steels. Their preferential dissolution can promote local 
acidification and chloride ion (Cl⁻) enrichment, thereby inducing localized destabilization of the passive film and 
facilitating pit initiation53.
Notably, scan rate exhibited only a weak partial correlation with pitting potential (r = − 0.042, 95% CI: −0.160 
to 0.056), with the confidence interval spanning zero. From an electrochemical testing perspective, scan rate 
is generally considered an important experimental parameter that can influence the measured pitting potential. 
However, the present result indicates that no robust independent linear association between scan rate and pitting 
potential was evident in the heterogeneous cross-literature dataset. Higher scan rates may cause the polariza -
tion process to bypass certain stages of metastable pit development, thereby resulting in artificially elevated Epit 
values. However, in the present large-scale cross-literature statistical analysis, the overall association of scan 
rate remains relatively limited. This observation may be attributed to the fact that scan rate primarily reflects 
methodological variations in experimental procedures rather than the intrinsic pitting corrosion resistance of the 
material itself. Furthermore, substantial differences often exist among studies with respect to electrode dimen -
sions, potential scan ranges, IR compensation procedures, stabilization times, and test termination criteria. The 
combined influence of these factors may reduce the independent statistical association of scan rate within the 
overall dataset. Consequently, compared with experimental parameters, alloy composition and environmental 
conditions remain the dominant factors governing the pitting corrosion behavior of stainless steels.
In addition, chloride ion concentration exhibits only a weak negative partial correlation with pitting potential 
(r = − 0.031, 95% CI: −0.147 to 0.070), with the confidence interval spanning zero. This result should not be 
interpreted as evidence that chloride ions (Cl⁻) play an insignificant role in pitting corrosion. Rather, it suggests 
that pitting behavior is governed by the coupled effects of multiple interacting factors rather than by chloride con-
centration alone. Within the large-scale literature dataset, chloride concentration often varies simultaneously with 
other influential parameters, including temperature, pH, cation species, and buffering systems. Consequently, its 
independent association may be partially diminished in the partial correlation analysis. Furthermore, the influence 
of chloride concentration on pitting corrosion may exhibit a threshold-like behavior. Once a critical concentration

is exceeded, further increases in chloride concentration may produce a progressively smaller effect on the pitting 
potential. As a result, chloride concentration does not exhibit a particularly strong independent correlation in the 
cross-system statistical analysis conducted in this study.
It should be noted that the observed partial correlations were generally weak in magnitude, with the largest 
absolute coefficient being 0.300. Therefore, these results should not be interpreted as evidence of strong predic -
tive or causal relationships. Rather, they indicate modest conditional associations within a heterogeneous cross-
literature dataset involving diverse alloy systems and experimental conditions. The publication-clustered 95% 
confidence intervals further characterize the uncertainty associated with these estimates.
Overall, the partial correlation analysis results are broadly consistent with established theories of pitting cor -
rosion in stainless steels, while also highlighting the differences between large-scale cross-literature statistical 
observations and idealized experimental systems. Although the observed associations are modest in magnitude, 
their general consistency with established corrosion knowledge provides a scientific plausibility check for the 
statistical patterns captured by the constructed database.
Conclusion
This study developed an LLM-based framework for structured data extraction and database construction from 
scientific literature and validated its effectiveness using stainless steel pitting corrosion research as a case study. 
By decomposing the complex information extraction process into multiple subtasks, including experimental con-
dition extraction, pitting potential extraction, and data alignment, the proposed framework enabled the automated 
acquisition and integration of experimental data from large-scale unstructured scientific literature.
Using a broad candidate corpus of 12,792 publications, the framework generated a structured extraction out -
put containing 36,095 experimental records, from which material-screened subsets were subsequently used for 
stainless-steel-specific analyses. The results demonstrate that the proposed framework is capable of effectively 
performing structured information extraction from complex scientific texts, with strong aggregate field-level 
performance but variable accuracy across individual extraction tasks. In particular, stage-specific validation iden-
tified pitting-potential extraction and normalization as comparatively challenging components of the workflow. 
These findings indicate that the framework provides a practical and scalable approach for high-throughput data -
base construction in corrosion science, while emphasizing the importance of considering field-specific extraction 
uncertainty in downstream analyses.
Building upon the constructed database, statistical analyses were subsequently conducted. The results indicate 
that the material-screened stainless-steel dataset is predominantly characterized by room-temperature, neutral 
chloride-containing environments and austenitic and duplex stainless steel systems. Partial correlation analysis 
further revealed conditional associations between alloy composition, environmental factors, and pitting potential. 
Among the investigated variables, molybdenum (Mo) exhibited the strongest positive conditional association, 
whereas temperature showed a negative conditional association. In contrast, chloride ion concentration showed 
only a weak negative association. These statistical patterns are generally consistent with established corrosion 
knowledge, providing a scientific plausibility check while also highlighting differences between large-scale 
cross-literature statistical associations and traditional empirical models.
Overall, this study demonstrates the capability of LLMs for knowledge extraction and automated database 
construction from materials science literature and confirms their feasibility for applications in corrosion infor -
matics. The proposed framework provides an effective pathway for transforming large volumes of unstructured 
scientific literature into structured, machine-readable data resources, thereby facilitating large-scale data analysis 
and knowledge discovery in corrosion science. The primary objective of the present study is database construc -
tion and validation, with the partial correlation analysis serving as an initial demonstration of the analytical 
utility of the resulting database. Future studies may further exploit this data resource using advanced data-
driven approaches, such as clustering, machine learning, and other multivariate techniques, to identify complex

corrosion patterns and develop predictive models for pitting behavior. In addition, advances in multimodal LLMs, 
knowledge graphs, and materials ontology technologies may further enhance the capabilities of the proposed 
framework and enable the integration of diverse corrosion-related data sources, including polarization curves, 
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data, microstructural information, and surface morphology char-
acterization results. These developments would support more comprehensive corrosion knowledge integration 
and data-driven discovery, providing new opportunities for materials design, performance prediction, and intel -
ligent scientific research.
